Why social-emotional learning matters

Social-emotional learning (SEL) is the ongoing process of developing skills to understand and manage emotions, show empathy, maintain supportive relationships, and make caring, responsible decisions. These skills support participation in learning, peer relationships, and well-being.

SEL is teachable. Children learn through direct instruction, adult modeling, play, discussion, practice, and relationships. SEL is not a demand that children stay calm all the time; it is support for noticing feelings, asking for help, and choosing a next step.

How early experiences can shape later development

Development starts before birth and continues through adolescence. Prenatal health and care—including access to health care and support for a pregnant person’s physical and mental health—are part of the conditions that can influence a child’s early development. Research also links prenatal stress and perinatal depression or anxiety with later developmental outcomes at a group level.

After birth, responsive caregiving, safe and stable relationships, opportunities to play and communicate, and access to needed supports help children develop trust, emotional understanding, and coping skills. Persistent adversity or stress can make regulation and relationships more challenging for some children. These are influences, not predictions: children develop differently, and supportive relationships and appropriate supports can strengthen well-being at every age.

Use a strengths-based lens. Do not blame a child or family for developmental differences or stressors. Offer respectful partnership, practical support, and referrals when a family wants them.

Three developmental stages: milestones and classroom practice

Milestones are guideposts, not a scorecard. Children may show skills at different times, and culture, language, disability, temperament, and experience all matter. Observe each child over time and partner with caregivers when concerns arise.

About ages 3–5

Early childhood

Belonging, play, emotion words, and learning with others

Examples of social-emotional milestones
  • Notices other children and may join their play.
  • Begins taking turns and following simple game rules with support.
  • Shows growing independence and can take part in simple routines or chores.
  • Begins to name feelings and may need an adult’s help to calm down or solve a conflict.
Two classroom activities

Feelings faces and stories

Read a picture book, pause to notice characters’ faces and body clues, then invite children to name or point to a feeling. Accept words, gestures, pictures, or communication devices.

Cooperative play challenge

Invite pairs or small groups to build a shared structure or complete a simple art project. Model phrases such as “Can I have a turn?” and “Let’s make a plan.”

About ages 6–11

Middle childhood

Friendships, perspective-taking, confidence, and problem-solving

Examples of social-emotional milestones
  • Builds stronger, more complex friendships and increasingly values peer belonging.
  • Develops a stronger sense of responsibility and independence.
  • Can increasingly recognize different perspectives and discuss fairness, while still needing help with conflicts.
  • Practices managing frustration, waiting, and adjusting to group expectations; these skills are still developing.
Two classroom activities

Class meeting and problem-solving circle

Use a predictable meeting to discuss a shared classroom challenge. Students practice listening, stating needs respectfully, and agreeing on one achievable next step.

Teamwork reflection

After a partner task, ask: “What helped us work together?” and “What could we try next time?” Let students respond verbally, in writing, or with a visual scale.

About ages 12–18

Adolescence

Identity, agency, belonging, and increasingly independent decisions

Examples of social-emotional milestones
  • Explores identity, values, interests, and future goals.
  • Often seeks greater independence and may place increased importance on peer relationships.
  • Experiences a wide range of emotions and may be learning to manage overlapping or intense feelings.
  • Develops more complex reflection and decision-making skills, while still benefiting from trusted adults and support.
Two classroom activities

Values-to-action reflection

Students privately identify values that matter to them, then choose a school or community goal that reflects one value. Sharing is optional; protect privacy and avoid forced disclosure.

Scenario-based decision practice

Use a fictional peer or online scenario. Students identify choices, possible outcomes, who could help, and a response aligned with safety and personal values.

Self-regulation: what it is and how to support it

A practical definition

Self-regulation is the developing ability to notice and manage feelings, attention, thoughts, and behavior in ways that fit a situation and support a goal. It does not mean suppressing emotion or always sitting still. Children often need adults to co-regulate with them before they can use a strategy independently.

What it can look like

  • A student notices, “I’m getting frustrated,” and asks for help.
  • A child uses a break card, movement break, or quiet space and then returns when ready.
  • A student pauses before responding to a disagreement and tries a respectful phrase.
  • A learner uses a visual checklist to begin and complete a task.

Three ways teachers can model and encourage regulation

  1. Think aloud and name your own strategy. For example: “I’m feeling rushed, so I’m going to take one slow breath, check my list, and do the first step.” Model calm repair when you make a mistake.
  2. Teach and rehearse tools when students are calm. Practice emotion words, breathing, stretching, asking for help, using a break signal, and choosing a next step. Offer more than one option; not every strategy works for every child.
  3. Design the classroom to reduce avoidable stress. Use predictable routines, visual directions, clear transitions, brief directions, movement opportunities, positive feedback, and access to a quiet or low-stimulation space.
When a child is dysregulated: Prioritize safety and connection over correction. Use a calm voice, few words, and a non-threatening posture. Reduce demands and stimulation when possible; offer space and two simple choices. Avoid arguing, shaming, or requiring the child to explain while highly upset. When the child is calm, reconnect, identify what helped, practice a replacement skill, and plan for next time. Follow the student’s support plan and school safety procedures.

For children with ADHD or other learning and developmental differences: Regulation challenges are not automatically willful misbehavior. Individualize supports with the student and family, use consistent cues and positive reinforcement, make movement and sensory options available, and coordinate with the school support team. Do not assume that one strategy works for every child.

Partnering with families: ideas to try at home

Invite families to adapt strategies to their language, culture, routines, resources, and child’s preferences. Share what works at school and ask caregivers what helps at home.

Early childhood

  • Name feelings during everyday moments: “You look disappointed that playtime ended.”
  • Use simple picture books, pretend play, and puppets to act out asking for a turn or getting help.
  • Keep routines predictable where possible; give a brief warning before transitions.
  • Offer two manageable choices and notice effort: “You were upset and came to sit with me.”

Middle childhood

  • Make time for a low-pressure daily check-in; listening can come before problem-solving.
  • Practice a short plan for tough moments: notice the feeling, choose a tool, and decide what to do next.
  • Let the child help with age-appropriate responsibilities and reflect on what went well.
  • Talk through friendship conflicts by considering each person’s perspective and possible repair.

Adolescence

  • Listen without immediately judging or fixing; ask, “Would you like advice, help brainstorming, or just someone to listen?”
  • Support privacy and growing independence while maintaining clear, caring safety boundaries.
  • Encourage healthy ways to manage stress—movement, rest, creative activities, time with supportive people, or a trusted adult.
  • Discuss decisions using values, safety, likely outcomes, and available support rather than relying only on lectures.
When families have concerns: Encourage them to talk with the child’s health care provider or school support team. Developmental milestones are not diagnostic tools. A family’s questions deserve respectful attention and, when appropriate, professional screening or evaluation.
